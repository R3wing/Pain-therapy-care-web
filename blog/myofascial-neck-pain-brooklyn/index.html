Muscle Knots and Chronic Myofascial Neck Pain: Diagnosis and Non-Surgical Options

By Pain Therapy Medical Care · Updated October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

TL;DR

Persistent muscle knots in the neck and upper back are usually myofascial trigger points, tight bands of muscle that can cause ongoing local and referred pain. Some common treatments have weaker evidence than people assume.

  • Trigger points can refer pain to areas away from the knot itself, including the head.
  • Ultrasound therapy alone is not more effective than placebo for this condition.
  • Manual therapy, exercise, and dry needling have better supporting evidence.
  • Chronic, unresolved trigger points are worth a proper evaluation rather than repeated massage alone.

If you have persistent tight bands or “knots” in your neck and upper back that keep coming back no matter how much you stretch or get a massage, here is what’s actually happening and what treatments the evidence actually supports.

What exactly is a myofascial trigger point?

A myofascial trigger point is a tight, hyperirritable band within a muscle that is tender to touch and can cause both local pain at the knot itself and referred pain felt elsewhere, often in a predictable pattern for a given muscle. In the neck and upper back, trigger points commonly develop in the upper trapezius, the muscle running from your neck to your shoulder, and can refer pain up into the head, contributing to tension-type headaches in some patients. This is different from a simple, temporary muscle tightness, since trigger points can persist for weeks or months without resolving on their own.

For general background, see this guide to neck pain treatment in Brooklyn.

What causes myofascial trigger points to develop and persist?

Common contributing factors include repetitive strain, sustained poor posture, stress-related muscle tension, and underlying issues elsewhere in the neck or shoulder that change how you move and load these muscles. Once a trigger point forms, a cycle can develop where the tight band itself changes local blood flow and muscle function in a way that makes the knot self-sustaining, which is part of why these can be so persistent even when the original triggering activity has stopped.

Which treatments actually have evidence behind them?

This is worth being honest about, since not every commonly used treatment for trigger points has strong supporting evidence. Manual therapies, hands-on techniques performed by a trained provider, combined with a structured exercise program, have reasonable evidence and are commonly recommended as a core part of treatment. Dry needling, a technique using a thin needle inserted directly into the trigger point, has evidence supporting reduced pain specifically for trigger points associated with neck pain. Laser therapy has shown some short-term benefit for reducing pain intensity, though the evidence for longer-term effects is still developing. Whether it helps when neck pain is severe is examined in cold laser therapy for severe neck pain.

Which common treatments have weaker evidence than people assume?

Conventional ultrasound therapy, despite being widely used, has not been shown to be more effective than placebo for myofascial trigger point pain in the neck and upper back according to the available research. Trigger point injections, meaning an injection directly into the knot, also have mixed evidence, without clear proof of benefit over other approaches regardless of what medication is used in the injection. This does not mean these treatments never help any individual patient, but it does mean they shouldn’t be assumed to be the most effective option available.

Do muscle knots ever indicate something more serious?

Most myofascial trigger points are a benign, if persistent, muscular issue. That said, chronic neck and upper back tightness that doesn’t respond to appropriate treatment is worth a proper evaluation to rule out other contributing factors, like an underlying cervical spine issue or postural strain from another source, such as prolonged desk work. A trigger point that developed alongside new numbness, tingling, or weakness is not typical for a simple muscular knot and warrants prompt evaluation.

What does a realistic treatment plan look like?

A well-supported plan generally combines manual therapy or dry needling for the trigger points themselves with a structured exercise program addressing strength and movement patterns in the neck and shoulder, since exercise alone or combined with manual therapy has some of the better supporting evidence for lasting improvement, not just short-term relief. Addressing contributing factors, like workstation setup or stress management, is often part of a complete plan as well, since treating the trigger point without addressing what’s driving it tends to lead to the knots coming back.

How is myofascial pain different from cervical radiculopathy or tech neck?

Myofascial pain is centered on the muscle itself, palpable tight bands with local tenderness and a characteristic referred pain pattern, rather than the burning, electric, or numb sensations that typically indicate a nerve is involved, as with a pinched nerve. It also differs from general tech neck fatigue, which tends to be more diffuse and directly tied to sustained positioning, without the same discrete, palpable knots. These conditions can overlap or coexist, which is another reason a proper exam, rather than self-diagnosis, is worth doing when symptoms persist. Frequently asked questions

Is dry needling the same as acupuncture?
They use similar tools but are based on different principles. Dry needling specifically targets identified trigger points based on Western medical understanding of muscle physiology.

Can stress really cause muscle knots?
Yes, sustained muscle tension related to stress is a recognized contributing factor, particularly in the neck and upper trapezius.

Will massage alone fix this long-term?
Massage can provide temporary relief, but for persistent trigger points, combining treatment with a structured exercise program tends to produce more lasting results.

How long do trigger points typically take to resolve?
It varies by individual and how long the trigger point has been present, but many patients notice improvement within several sessions of an appropriately targeted plan.

What is a muscle knot in the neck, physically?
The term describes a taut, tender band within muscle that reproduces familiar pain when pressed and can refer that pain elsewhere, often into the head or shoulder blade. It is a clinical finding rather than a visible lesion, which is why examination rather than imaging identifies it.
