How to Find the Best Pain Management Doctor in Brooklyn

By Pain Therapy Medical Care · Updated October 22, 2026 · 4 min read

Summary

The right pain management doctor finds the cause of your pain, offers more than one way to treat it, and explains the plan clearly.

  • Check board certification and training in pain medicine or a related specialty.
  • Look for a range of treatment options, not a single procedure for everyone.
  • Confirm insurance, location, hours and how easy it is to reach the office.
  • Be cautious of guaranteed results, pressure to commit to long packages, or opioid-only plans.

What does a pain management doctor do?

A pain management doctor diagnoses the source of ongoing pain and builds a treatment plan. Plans can include physical therapy, medication, injections, device-based therapies and lifestyle changes. They often coordinate with your primary care doctor and other specialists. See what a pain management doctor does.

What credentials should you look for?

  • Board certification in pain medicine, or in a related specialty such as physical medicine and rehabilitation (PM&R), anesthesiology or neurology, often with fellowship training in pain management.
  • A medical degree: MD (Doctor of Medicine) or DO (Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine). Both are fully licensed physicians.
  • A valid New York license, which you can check on the New York State Office of the Professions website.

What treatment options should a good practice offer?

Chronic pain rarely has a single fix. A strong practice can offer or coordinate several approaches:

  • Physical and occupational therapy
  • Image-guided injections when appropriate
  • Non-drug device-based therapies, such as shockwave or laser therapy
  • Medication management that limits long-term opioid use
  • Referral to surgeons or other specialists when needed

A practice with fewer tools that explains its limits and refers when needed can serve you better than one that offers everything and gives everyone the same plan.

How should the doctor approach diagnosis?

  • A detailed history: where it hurts, how long, what makes it better or worse
  • A hands-on physical exam
  • A review of earlier imaging and treatments
  • New tests only when they will change the plan
  • A clear explanation of the likely cause and the reasoning behind the plan

What practical things matter?

  • Your insurance is accepted, checked before your first visit
  • A location you can reach for follow-up visits
  • Hours that fit your schedule
  • Easy contact by phone or online
  • Reasonable wait times for a first appointment

What are the red flags?

  • Promises of a guaranteed cure
  • Pressure to buy large packages of sessions up front
  • The same treatment recommended for every patient
  • A plan built mainly around long-term opioids
  • Little time spent on your history or exam

What questions should you ask at the first visit?

  • What do you think is causing my pain?
  • What are my options, including non-drug options?
  • What results are realistic, and how long will it take?
  • What will my insurance cover, and what will I pay?
  • When should I expect to see improvement, and what happens if I do not?

Frequently asked questions

What kind of doctor is best for chronic pain?

A physician board certified in pain medicine or a related specialty such as physical medicine and rehabilitation or anesthesiology, who looks for the cause and offers several treatment options.

Is a DO as qualified as an MD for pain management?

Yes. Both are fully licensed physicians who complete residency and can train and certify in pain medicine.

Do I need a referral to see a pain management doctor?

Not with Original Medicare or many PPO plans. Many HMO, Medicare Advantage and Medicaid plans require one.

How do I know if a pain clinic is good?

Look for board-certified doctors, a clear diagnosis, a range of options, honest timelines and no pressure to commit to large packages.

What should I bring to my first pain management visit?

Your ID and insurance card, a list of medications, earlier imaging or reports, and notes on what has and has not helped.
