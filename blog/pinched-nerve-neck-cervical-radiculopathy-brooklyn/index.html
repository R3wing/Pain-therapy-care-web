Pinched Nerve in the Neck: Cervical Radiculopathy Symptoms and Treatment

By Pain Therapy Medical Care · Updated September 18, 2026 · 6 min read

Summary

A pinched nerve in the neck, called cervical radiculopathy, causes pain, tingling, numbness, or weakness that radiates from the neck into the arm or hand. Most cases improve with non-surgical care.

  • Symptoms typically follow a specific pattern down one arm, not both.
  • Weakness or significant numbness is a reason to be evaluated promptly.
  • Most people improve with conservative treatment over several weeks.
  • Imaging is not always needed right away and depends on your exam findings.

If you have neck pain that radiates into your arm, along with tingling, numbness, or weakness, you may be dealing with a pinched nerve in your neck. Here is what causes it and what treatment typically looks like.

What does a pinched nerve in the neck actually mean?

A pinched nerve in the neck, medically called cervical radiculopathy, happens when a nerve root exiting your cervical spine becomes irritated or compressed. This can result from a herniated disc pressing on the nerve, bone spurs from osteoarthritis narrowing the space the nerve passes through, or general degenerative changes in the neck. The irritated nerve then causes symptoms along the specific arm pathway that nerve serves.

What are the symptoms of a pinched nerve in the neck?

Common symptoms include neck pain that radiates into the shoulder, arm, or hand, often on one side only, along with tingling, numbness, or a burning sensation following that same pathway. Some people also notice weakness in specific arm or hand movements, or a change in reflexes on the affected side. The specific pattern of symptoms, which fingers are affected, which movements feel weak, helps your provider identify which nerve level is involved.

How is a pinched nerve in the neck diagnosed?

Diagnosis starts with a history and physical exam, including specific tests of arm strength, reflexes, and sensation, along with maneuvers that can reproduce your symptoms when a nerve root is involved. Imaging, usually an MRI, may be used to confirm the diagnosis and identify the specific cause, but it is not always needed immediately. Your exam findings guide whether and when imaging is appropriate, particularly if your symptoms are mild and improving.

What does treatment for a pinched nerve typically involve?

Most cases of cervical radiculopathy improve with non-surgical care. This typically includes activity modification, guided physical therapy focused on specific neck positions and exercises, and short-term pain management strategies. Some patients also use adjuncts like laser therapy as part of a broader plan, though the evidence for this is more established for acute symptoms than for guaranteed long-term outcomes, and it is not a substitute for the exercise-based foundation of care.

Injections, including epidural steroid injections near the affected nerve root, are sometimes considered for patients whose symptoms have not responded well enough to initial conservative treatment. Surgery is generally reserved for cases with significant, persistent symptoms, meaningful weakness, or when conservative care has not provided enough relief over a reasonable trial period.

How is a pinched nerve different from ordinary neck stiffness?

Ordinary neck stiffness or muscle strain usually stays localized to the neck and shoulder area, without radiating symptoms into the arm or hand. It also does not typically cause numbness, tingling, or weakness in a specific pattern. Cervical radiculopathy is different because the nerve root itself is irritated, so symptoms travel along that nerve’s pathway rather than staying at the site of the original irritation. This is one reason a proper evaluation matters: treating a pinched nerve like a simple muscle strain, with rest and heat alone, can leave the underlying nerve irritation unaddressed.

Can work or daily activities make a pinched nerve worse?

Certain positions and activities can aggravate cervical radiculopathy while the nerve root is irritated. Prolonged looking down at a phone or screen, sleeping in awkward positions, and repetitive overhead reaching can all increase symptoms for some people. This does not mean you need to avoid all activity. Your provider can help you identify which specific positions tend to reproduce your symptoms so you can modify those while staying otherwise active, since complete inactivity is not typically recommended either.

How long does it take to recover from a pinched nerve in the neck?

Many people see meaningful improvement within four to six weeks of starting appropriate conservative treatment, though this varies by individual and by how significant the nerve irritation is. Some symptoms, particularly numbness or tingling, can take longer to fully resolve than pain does, even as the underlying irritation improves. Your provider can give you a more specific expectation based on your exam and how you respond to initial treatment.

When should I seek care right away?

Seek prompt medical evaluation for significant or worsening arm weakness, loss of hand coordination, or any new bowel or bladder changes. These symptoms can indicate a more significant nerve or spinal cord issue that needs timely assessment rather than a wait-and-see approach.

Frequently asked questions

Is a pinched nerve the same as a herniated disc?
A herniated disc is one common cause of a pinched nerve, but not the only one. Bone spurs and other degenerative changes can also compress a nerve root.

Will I need surgery for a pinched nerve in my neck?
Most people do not. Surgery is generally reserved for cases with significant weakness or symptoms that have not responded to a reasonable trial of conservative treatment.

Why does my hand feel numb if the problem is in my neck?
Nerves that originate in your neck travel down into your arm and hand. Irritation at the neck can cause symptoms anywhere along that nerve’s pathway, including your fingers.

How is a pinched nerve in the neck confirmed?
Examination usually comes first, testing reflexes, strength and sensation in specific patterns that correspond to individual nerve roots. Imaging or nerve conduction studies are added when the findings are unclear, when weakness is present, or when symptoms are not settling as expected.

Can physical therapy make a pinched nerve worse?
Properly guided physical therapy is a core part of treatment, not something that typically worsens symptoms. Your provider will tailor exercises to avoid positions that aggravate your specific symptoms.
