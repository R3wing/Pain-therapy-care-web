If numbness, tingling, or burning in your feet or hands is affecting your daily life and you live in the Monsey or Five Towns area, you have access to evaluation and treatment close to home.

What symptoms suggest I should be evaluated for neuropathy?

Numbness, tingling, or a burning sensation that typically starts in the toes and feet, sometimes described as a “stocking” pattern, is the most common presentation. Some patients also notice balance changes, increased sensitivity to touch, or difficulty feeling small objects with their hands as symptoms progress. If these symptoms are new, worsening, or affecting your daily activities, an evaluation can help identify the cause and start appropriate treatment before symptoms progress further.

What causes neuropathy?

Diabetes is one of the most common causes of peripheral neuropathy, but it is far from the only one. Certain medications, vitamin deficiencies, and other underlying health conditions can also contribute, and in some cases, a specific cause is never identified even after a thorough evaluation. Identifying the underlying cause when possible matters, since managing that cause, like blood sugar control for diabetic neuropathy, is often a central part of an effective treatment plan. What does an evaluation for neuropathy involve?

An evaluation includes a detailed history of your symptoms and relevant medical history, along with a physical exam checking sensation, reflexes, and strength in the affected areas. Depending on your history and exam findings, your provider may recommend blood work to check for underlying causes, or nerve conduction studies and electromyography to assess how your nerves are functioning. By the end of the visit, you should have a clearer picture of what is likely causing your symptoms and what your treatment options look like.

What does treatment for neuropathy typically involve?

Treatment starts with addressing any identifiable underlying cause, then focuses on managing symptoms and function. Depending on your evaluation, this can include medications aimed at neuropathic pain specifically, and for appropriate candidates, non-surgical technologies like laser therapy as part of a broader plan. If balance or fall risk is a concern, physical therapy focused on those specific issues may also be part of your plan. What the research actually supports is examined in whether laser therapy helps neuropathy.

What does a first visit for neuropathy involve?

Your first visit centers on a full evaluation: a review of your symptom history, a physical exam checking sensation, reflexes, and strength, and a discussion of what has and has not helped so far. If you have prior blood work, imaging, or records related to your symptoms, bring them, since they can help build a more complete picture from the start. By the end of the visit, you should have a clearer sense of what is likely driving your symptoms and what your treatment options look like.

Frequently asked questions

Does neuropathy pain feel different from muscle or joint pain?
Usually. Nerve pain is more often described as burning, electrical, stabbing or pins and needles, and it can appear without any movement triggering it. Muscle and joint pain tends to be duller, more localized, and clearly worse with particular movements or positions.

Can neuropathy affect balance as well as sensation?
Yes. When nerves carrying position information from the feet are affected, the brain receives less reliable input about where the feet are, which can make standing and walking feel less steady, particularly in the dark. Balance changes are worth reporting because they raise the priority of fall prevention.

Will neuropathy symptoms go away completely?
It varies by cause and how early treatment starts. Your provider can give you a realistic expectation based on your specific evaluation.

Are nerve tests needed before neuropathy treatment starts?
Not always. Pattern, distribution and history often identify the problem without formal testing. Nerve conduction studies or blood work become more useful when the cause is unclear, when weakness is present, or when symptoms are progressing in a way that could point to something requiring different management.

Is neuropathy always related to diabetes?
No. While diabetes is a common cause, neuropathy can result from several other causes, or occasionally no clear cause is identified.
