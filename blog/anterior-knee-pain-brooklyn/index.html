Pain in the Front of the Knee: Why Stairs, Squats, and Running Hurt

By Pain Therapy Medical Care · Updated October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

If pain in the front of your knee flares up specifically with stairs, squats, or running, you’re likely dealing with a specific and well-studied condition, not a mystery injury. Here is what’s actually going on and what helps.

What is causing pain in the front of my knee?

Pain around or behind the kneecap that is aggravated by weight-bearing, bent-knee activities, like squatting, stair climbing, running, or jumping, is most often patellofemoral pain, sometimes still called anterior knee pain, runner’s knee, or patellofemoral pain syndrome. These are different names for essentially the same clinical condition. It is one of the most common knee complaints seen in sports medicine and pain clinics, and it typically comes on gradually rather than from a single injury, often following a noticeable increase in activities like running, jumping, or repetitive squatting.

For a broader overview, see this guide to knee pain treatment in Brooklyn.

Why does it flare specifically with stairs and squats?

These activities load the kneecap against the thigh bone at a bent-knee angle, which is exactly the position that tends to aggravate this condition. Going downstairs, in particular, places more load through the front of the knee than going upstairs does, which is why many patients notice their pain is worse descending than ascending. This pattern, pain specifically tied to bent-knee, weight-bearing activities, is itself one of the more useful diagnostic clues.

How is patellofemoral pain actually diagnosed?

Diagnosis is largely clinical, based on your history and a physical exam, rather than relying on imaging. Current clinical guidelines identify reproducing your familiar pain during a squat as one of the most useful findings for confirming the diagnosis. Your provider will also check your knee’s alignment, muscle strength, particularly around the hip and thigh, and movement patterns during functional tasks like squatting or stepping down, since these all inform the underlying contributing factors specific to you. When the source turns out to sit above the knee entirely, the pattern is explained in how the hip can refer pain to the knee.

Do I need an MRI to confirm this diagnosis?

Generally, no. Imaging such as an MRI is not typically helpful for identifying patellofemoral pain and is not part of standard diagnostic workup for straightforward cases. Imaging becomes more relevant if your history or exam findings suggest a different diagnosis, like a meniscus tear or a ligament injury, or if your symptoms don’t respond as expected to appropriate treatment. For most patients, a thorough clinical exam is sufficient to make the diagnosis and start treatment.

What treatment actually helps?

Current evidence-based guidelines point most strongly toward targeted exercise, particularly strengthening of the hip and thigh muscles, as the foundation of treatment. Weakness in the hip muscles that control leg alignment during activities like squatting and running is commonly involved in this condition, and addressing that weakness directly tends to produce better outcomes than rest alone. Other approaches, like taping, bracing, or activity modification, may be used as supporting strategies alongside a structured strengthening program, but they are not typically effective as standalone treatments. How those findings translate into a treatment sequence is set out in how to approach knee pain treatment.

How long does it typically take to improve?

Timelines vary depending on how long you’ve had symptoms and how consistently you follow a targeted program, but many patients notice meaningful improvement within several weeks of starting appropriate treatment. This condition often responds well to consistent conservative care, and your provider can give you a realistic expectation based on your specific findings and activity goals, whether that’s returning to recreational running or simply managing daily stairs without pain.

What role does footwear or running form play?

Footwear and running form can be contributing factors for some patients, but they are generally considered secondary to strength deficits in current evidence-based guidelines. Rather than assuming a shoe change or a form overhaul will resolve symptoms on its own, most guidelines point to addressing hip and thigh strength first, with footwear or gait adjustments considered as supporting strategies for patients who don’t fully respond to a strengthening program alone or who have a specific identifiable gait pattern contributing to their symptoms.

Frequently asked questions

Is patellofemoral pain the same as a meniscus tear?
No. They are different conditions with different exam findings, and imaging or a specific physical exam can help distinguish between them if there’s uncertainty.

Should I stop running completely?
Not necessarily. Activity modification, rather than complete rest, combined with targeted strengthening, is generally the more effective approach for most patients.

Will this come back after it improves?
It can, particularly if activity increases again without adequate strength to support it. Maintaining the strengthening work that resolved your symptoms helps reduce that risk.

Does my hip really matter if my pain is in my knee?
Yes. Hip and thigh strength directly affects how your kneecap tracks during activity, which is why hip-focused strengthening is often central to treatment even though the pain is felt at the knee.

Why does front-of-knee pain get worse on stairs and squats?
Those movements increase compression between the kneecap and the thigh bone, so any problem with how the kneecap tracks or how load is shared becomes symptomatic. Walking on level ground loads the joint far less, which is why pain can disappear entirely on the flat.
