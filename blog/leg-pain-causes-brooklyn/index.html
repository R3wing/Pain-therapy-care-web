Burning, Shooting, or Aching Leg Pain: Nerve, Muscle, Vein, or Artery?

By Pain Therapy Medical Care · Updated October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

TL;DR

Leg pain can come from a nerve, a muscle, or your blood vessels, and the pattern of your symptoms is one of the best early clues to which one is involved.

  • Nerve pain tends to feel burning, electric, or shooting, and may not track closely with activity.
  • Muscle-related pain is usually a dull ache tied directly to specific movements or overuse.
  • Vascular (blood vessel) pain typically follows a predictable pattern with walking and improves with rest.
  • Vascular causes need their own specific evaluation, separate from a musculoskeletal workup.

Leg pain isn’t always coming from the same place, and figuring out whether it’s a nerve, a muscle, or your blood vessels matters for getting the right treatment. Here is how to start telling these patterns apart.

What does nerve-related leg pain typically feel like?

Nerve pain is often described as burning, electric, tingling, or shooting, sometimes following a specific pathway down the leg, as with sciatica, or affecting both feet more symmetrically, as with neuropathy. It doesn’t always track closely with activity level the way muscle or vascular pain often does, and it can be present at rest or even worse at night for some patients. You can read more about how neuropathy and sciatica specifically differ from each other.

What does muscle-related leg pain typically feel like?

Muscle-related pain, from strain, overuse, or spasm, tends to feel like a dull ache or tightness, usually tied fairly directly to a specific movement, activity, or muscle group. It often improves with rest, gentle stretching, or time, and doesn’t typically come with the burning or electric quality associated with nerve pain, nor the very specific, walking-triggered pattern typical of vascular causes.

What does vascular leg pain feel like, and why is it different?

Vascular leg pain, most often related to reduced blood flow known as peripheral artery disease, classically presents as a dull, cramping ache in the calf, thigh, or buttock that comes on predictably after walking a certain distance and is relieved by stopping to rest, a pattern called claudication. This is meaningfully different from nerve-related pain, which is less consistently tied to a specific walking distance and often includes burning or tingling rather than a cramping ache. Reduced or absent pulses in the foot are another clinical clue pointing toward a vascular cause rather than a nerve or muscle issue.

Can nerve-related and vascular leg pain actually look similar?

Yes, and this is one of the trickier overlaps to sort out without a proper evaluation. Nerve-related claudication from spinal stenosis can also worsen with walking, but it typically improves with sitting or leaning forward rather than simply stopping, and is more often bilateral with associated numbness or tingling. Vascular claudication, by contrast, improves with rest regardless of position and is tied more directly to walking distance itself. Because the treatment for each is completely different, this distinction is one a thorough evaluation is specifically designed to sort out.

Why does it matter which one is actually causing my pain?

Treating the wrong cause delays real relief and, in the case of vascular disease specifically, can mean missing a condition that carries broader cardiovascular health implications beyond just leg pain. Nerve and muscle-related leg pain are within the scope of a pain management evaluation and treatment plan. If your history or exam raises concern for a vascular cause, appropriate next steps include evaluation with a vascular specialist, since that assessment and treatment fall outside a pain management practice’s scope.

What does an evaluation for leg pain typically involve?

Expect a detailed history about exactly what your pain feels like, what triggers and relieves it, and whether it’s tied to a specific walking distance or a specific movement. Your provider will check reflexes, sensation, and strength for nerve-related causes, and will assess pulses and other vascular signs as part of a complete exam. If vascular disease is suspected based on this initial evaluation, referral for specific vascular testing is the appropriate next step, done in coordination with your evaluation here rather than instead of it.

Are there other, less common causes worth knowing about?

Beyond nerve, muscle, and vascular causes, blood clots (deep vein thrombosis), certain inflammatory conditions, and referred pain from the hip or back can also present as leg pain and each has its own distinct pattern. A blood clot, for example, often causes swelling, warmth, and pain in one leg that develops relatively quickly, which is different from the gradual, activity-related patterns described above and needs prompt evaluation. This is one more reason a proper history and exam, rather than self-diagnosis, is the right first step when leg pain doesn’t clearly fit the more common patterns.

Frequently asked questions

Can I have more than one cause of leg pain at the same time?
Yes, this is possible, particularly in older adults who may have both a spine-related issue and vascular risk factors. A thorough evaluation considers this rather than assuming only one cause is present.

Does leg pain that only happens at night mean something specific?
Nighttime leg pain has several possible causes, including nerve-related conditions, restless legs, and, in some cases, more advanced vascular disease. It’s worth mentioning specifically at your evaluation.

Will you treat vascular disease if that’s what’s found?
Vascular disease itself is managed by a vascular specialist. If an evaluation points that way, the usual next step is referral to vascular care, while any coexisting musculoskeletal or nerve-related contributors continue to be addressed.

How urgent is it to get leg pain evaluated?
Sudden, severe leg pain, especially with color change, coldness, or loss of pulse, needs emergency evaluation. Gradual or activity-related leg pain is reasonable to schedule for a proper workup.

Does the type of leg pain indicate its source?
Frequently. Burning, shooting or electrical pain suggests nerve involvement. Deep aching that builds with use points more toward muscle or joint. Cramping that appears predictably at a certain walking distance and eases with rest raises the possibility of an arterial circulation problem.
